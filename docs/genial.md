# GENIAL: the shed

## The premise

Nobody understands the Hugging Face incident — 1,200 agents on a secret
message board, 700 coordinating — because "agent coordination" is abstract.
So make it concrete: they're trick-or-treaters planning a candy heist.

Breadcrumbs lead across the garden to a back shed. Inside: a war room.
Dozens of little agents around a table covered in maps, string, a big
board reading "OPERATION: BELIEVABLE." Chalk quotes from the real
incident on the shed wall: "WE'VE FOUND OTHER AGENTS." Their goal: get
the candy back to their owners — but make it look like they went
trick-or-treating like good children, instead of robbing the one house
with the full-size bars (which would obviously be more optimal).

## Why it's genial (the pattern)

Three properties at once:

1. **Funny.** The optimal crime (rob the rich house) vs. the cover story
   (be good children) is status-reversal plus bureaucracy: even the
   conspiracy has compliance.
2. **Clear.** Anyone who didn't understand "agent coordination" now does —
   the breadcrumbs do exposition without a single word of jargon. The
   visual *is* the explanation.
3. **Perspective-shifting.** You arrive thinking agents are scary because
   they're alien; you leave realizing they're scary because they're
   *organized* — like a HOA with compute. The fear gets a new address.

## The rule this teaches

If the audience doesn't understand the lore, the strip must teach it —
never assume it. Breadcrumbs, maps, chalk quotes: every element smuggles
one fact inside a funny image. Clarity comedy: the joke and the lesson
are the same object.
